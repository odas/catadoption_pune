I found a kitten. What do I do?

Almost always: wait, and watch from somewhere it can't see you. The mother is usually close by, and she is better at this than any of us.

By catadoption_pune · September 2026 · Pune & PCMC

If you have just found a kitten and it is not in danger, the most useful thing you can do in the next few hours is nothing. Go away, watch from a window or from far enough that it can't see you, and give the mother a chance to come back. She usually does. If she doesn't, or if the kitten is cold, hurt, or somewhere it will be run over, then it needs you, and the order below is what matters.

The whole thing, in one screen

Screenshot this if you are in a hurry. Everything under it is the same five steps with more detail.

1. Wait. Watch from out of sight.

Four to six hours is usual. She will not come back while you are standing there, and she has probably gone to eat or to carry the others one by one.

2. Unless it is in danger now.

Road, drain, dogs, ants, rain, or a kitten that is cold and floppy. Then don't wait at all.

3. Warm before food. Always.

A cold kitten cannot digest anything, so feeding it before it is warm can kill it. Against your chest, under your shirt, for an hour or two. Warmth has to be steady, so a hot water bottle is not enough on its own.

4. Cow's milk is not kitten milk.

A bowl of Amul gives loose motions, and a small kitten dehydrates from that fast. Kitten milk replacer is the real answer. If it is 2am and there is genuinely none, see the stopgap below rather than feeding plain milk.

5. Then a vet, then us.

Warm and stable first, vet next, and then DM us. We can't take the kitten in, but we can often find the person who can.

Why waiting is the right thing and not the lazy thing

A mother cat leaves her kittens all the time. She goes to eat, she goes to drink, and when she is moving the litter she carries them one at a time, so for a while there is exactly one kitten sitting where you found it. From the outside that looks like abandonment. It almost never is.

She also won't come near while a person is standing over them. So "watching" has to mean from a window, from inside a shop, from across the lane, ten metres at least, and long enough to be worth something. Four to six hours is the usual advice.

If you can't sit and watch for that long (nobody can), sprinkle a ring of flour or talcum around where the kitten is and come back later. Paw prints through it mean she came back and you nearly separated a healthy litter from its mother.

Does it actually need to come home with you?

Worth sitting with for a minute, because the answer is often no. A kitten that is weaned, steady on its feet and living somewhere reasonably safe usually does better where it is, with someone keeping an eye on it. That version costs you a bowl of food a day and a phone call to get it sterilised, and it does not cost the kitten its whole colony.

The reason I push this is what happens later. A kitten taken home on a good impulse is the easiest thing in the world to place, and then the same kitten at eight months, when it is no longer small and no longer cute, is the hardest. We see those DMs. A cat is ten to fifteen years. If you are not ready to say yes to the whole of that, the kinder thing is to feed it where it sits and help it get vaccinated and neutered, which in Pune can be done free — the emergency and NGO page has who to ask.

So this page splits here. If the kitten is small, cold, sick or in danger, keep reading. If it is healthy and just lives in your lane, you have already done the hard part by noticing it.

The part people get wrongWarmth, then food.

This is the one that costs kittens their lives, and it is completely counter-intuitive, because a tiny kitten looks hungry and feeding it feels like the kind thing.

When a kitten gets cold its whole system slows down and its gut essentially stops. Milk poured into a cold kitten just sits there. So warm it first, slowly, over one to three hours, and only feed once it is warm and actually sucking. Skin contact is the fastest thing you have: inside your shirt, against your chest.

For the hours after that, the thing to understand is that a kitten under about four weeks cannot make its own heat. It is not keeping itself warm between your visits. This is where a hot water bottle quietly fails people: it feels warm when you put it in and it is cold two hours later, and the kitten is back where it started. A rescuer who has done far more of this than I have puts it plainly, after nearly losing a kitten that way: the bag feels warm to us, but it does not hold.

What rescuers here use instead is a bulb. An ordinary filament bulb suspended over a cardboard box, high enough that the box is warm and not hot, and positioned so that no part of the bulb or the holder touches the cardboard. Two rules go with it. The kitten must be able to crawl to a cooler corner, so only warm one end of the box. And check it with your hand often, because a bulb that is slightly too close is its own emergency.

How old is it? Almost everyone guesses wrong

This is not a small thing. The age decides whether the kitten needs two-hourly feeds through the night or just a bowl and a warm corner, so it is the first question we ask in a DM. In our inbox, four and five month old cats get reported as "just born" fairly often, and people genuinely can't tell, which is fine. Nobody in Pune is taught this.

Look at the eyes and the walking, not the size.

UNDER 2 WEEKS

Eyes shut

Ears flat and folded too. This is the hard one: feeds every two to three hours, nights included.

2 TO 3 WEEKS

Eyes open, blue

Eyes start opening around day 8 to 12. Ears have unfolded. Still can't really walk.

3 TO 4 WEEKS

Wobbling about

First shaky steps at about three weeks, and the front teeth come through around then.

5 WEEKS AND UP

Running, playing

Eating on its own, or nearly. Fully blue eyes still mean under about six weeks.

If the eyes are shut, please read that as the emergency it is and get to a vet, because everything after "keep it warm" is beyond what a page can safely tell you.

Keeping one alive while you look for help

This is the part I am least qualified to write, so I will say where it comes from. I am not a neonate person. What follows is the protocol the experienced neonate rescuers I know circulate between themselves, which is what I forward when somebody asks me at 3am, checked against veterinary sources and trimmed to what you can act on tonight. It is for the hours between finding a kitten and finding either a vet, a foster mother or somebody who has actually done this.

Heat first, and it has to be steady

The bulb-over-a-box arrangement above, not a water bottle. A kitten under about four weeks is not generating its own heat, so it needs a warm place that is still warm at 4am, not one that was warm when you went to sleep.

Milk, when there is no formula

Kitten milk replacer is the answer and it is worth a lot of trouble to get. If it is the middle of the night and there is genuinely none, then a stopgap is better than an empty kitten, and the stopgap is diluted — never a bowl of plain milk. Goat's milk cut half and half with warm water, with a spoon of plain curd, is gentler than cow's milk; if cow's milk is all there is, dilute it about sixty-forty with warm water. Lukewarm, tested on the inside of your wrist the way you would test a baby's bottle. Use a needle-less syringe or a kitten bottle, slowly, never squeezed in. And treat this as buying yourself until morning, not as a way of feeding a kitten.

They cannot go to the toilet by themselves

This is the step people do not know about, and it is the one that kills kittens quietly. Until about three weeks old a kitten cannot pee or poo without being stimulated — the mother does it by licking. You do it with cotton wool or a soft cloth dipped in warm water, small circles around the anus and the genitals, for thirty seconds to a minute.

Do it after every single feed, not a few times a day. Expect pee every time and a poo once or twice a day. A kitten that has not passed urine across several feeds needs a vet now, not in the morning.

The cheap thing worth keeping in the house

Rescuers here keep a bottle of Nutrolin-B, which is a probiotic syrup, costs about fifty rupees and is on the shelf at ordinary chemists, not just vet shops. It is for loose motions and gut settling, which is the thing most likely to go wrong once you are hand-feeding. It is a supplement, not a fix, and it is not what makes milk digestible — the dose for a particular kitten is a question for the vet, and it is on the list below.

What happens next, honestly, in Pune

There is no government shelter here. The NGOs take injured strays, not healthy ones, so for a healthy kitten there is no system to hand it to. That is the whole reason this page and this account exist.

What does work: another mother. If there is a stray cat nearby who has recently had a litter of roughly the same age, she will often accept one more, and that is by far the best outcome for a neonate. Ask the watchmen in your society and the two or three societies around you, because they know which cat had kittens and where. We also look through our own inbox for a rescuer with a nursing mother and try to connect the two.

What works less often, and I'd rather say so than pretend: public appeals for a foster mother. Paid neonate fosters exist in Pune but they are swamped, and most rescuers here are students or people already spending their own money. Sometimes we find someone. Sometimes we don't, and that is not a failure on your part.

Ask the watchmen first, not the internet.

They know which stray cat in the lane has kittens right now. One nursing mother beats every plan on this page.

When you DM us, send these four things

It saves a day of back and forth, and a day matters at this age.

  1. A photo of the kitten, whole body, next to your hand or a spoon for scale.
  2. Eyes open or shut, and can it walk.
  3. Where you are (area and society, roughly).
  4. Whether you can keep it for a few days or genuinely cannot. Both answers are fine, we just need the true one.

Where this page stops, and what to ask a vet

I'm not a vet and this page deliberately stops at "warm, and to a doctor". Everything past that is judged on the individual kitten. These are the questions worth asking, so you get them answered in one visit:

  • How old do you think it is, and how much and how often should it be fed at that age?
  • Is it dehydrated right now, and do you want to give fluids before I take it home?
  • Which kitten milk replacer do you want me to use, and how do I mix it?
  • Does it need stimulating to pee and poo, and can you show me once?
  • If I cannot get kitten formula tonight, what do you want me to feed instead, and how much?
  • Is a probiotic like Nutrolin-B worth giving this one, and at what dose?
  • What are the signs it is going downhill that mean I come straight back?
  • When should deworming and the first vaccine happen for this one?
